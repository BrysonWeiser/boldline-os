# Sales call playbook

**Bryson, 2026-08-17:** *"when I went on my sales call I realized there wasnt really a 'schedule'
to follow or steps to follow to close the call on the meeting."*

A 30-minute structure for the Calendly call, built to close on that call.

>
> **The rule, in one sentence: they pay the monthly minimum for their plan, or the fee their

> | $500 to $2,500 | $400 | $750 | not available |
> | $2,500 to $10,000 | $700 | $1,500 | $2,300 (needs $5,000+) |

> $1,500 once, no monthly, no per-lead fee, no contract. See the downsell script below.

## The one thing that shapes this whole script

**BoldLine has no clients yet.** So there are no results, no testimonials, no case studies, and
inventing any would be a lie that unravels the moment they ask a follow-up.

That takes the usual sales crutch away, and it is fine, because the strongest close available here
is better than social proof anyway: **make them do the math on their own numbers.** A roofer who
works out for himself that five extra jobs pays for a year of this does not need to hear about
someone else's roofer.

So the call is built around **their** numbers, not BoldLine's proof. Everything below serves that.

---

## Before the call (10 minutes)

1. **Run Deal Prep** on them in the OS. It researches the business and hands back talking points, a
   recommended package, and the per-lead math for their trade.
2. **Look at their website on your phone.** Is it fast? Does it have a form? Can you call them in
   one tap? You will reference this.
3. **Google their main service plus their city.** See who is already advertising. Those are the
   competitors eating their lunch right now, and naming two of them is the most credible thing you
   can say all call.
4. **Have open:** the OS, this playbook, their website, and that Google search.

## The 30 minutes

### 0 to 2 — Frame the call

Do not open with your background. Set the agenda and get permission to ask questions.

> "Thanks for making the time. Here's what I was thinking for the next 30 minutes. I'll ask you
> some questions about how you get customers today so I understand the business. Then if I think I
> can help I'll show you exactly what I'd do and what it costs. And if I don't think I can help,
> I'll tell you that too and we'll both get our time back. Sound fair?"

That last sentence does more work than anything else in the call. It tells them you are not going
to push, which makes them stop defending.

### 2 to 10 — Diagnose, and write the numbers down

**Ask, then shut up.** The biggest mistake on a call like this is filling silence. Their answers
become your close.

| Ask this | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| "Walk me through how a customer finds you today." | Tells you if they have any system at all |
| "What's a typical job worth to you?" | **The number the whole close runs on** |
| "How many more of those could you actually handle a month?" | Capacity. If the answer is zero, walk away |
| "Have you run Google ads before? What happened?" | Tells you what objection is coming |
| "When someone calls and you're on a roof, who picks up?" | **The real reason most ads fail** |
| "How fast do you usually get back to a new enquiry?" | Same. Sets up why the follow-up matters |
| "What would getting this fixed be worth to you?" | Their words, used later in the close |

**Write their average job value and their capacity number down.** You need both in six minutes.

### 10 to 14 — The math, out loud, on their numbers

This is the pivot. Do it slowly and let them arrive at it.

> "Let me just do some quick maths with your numbers, and tell me if I've got anything wrong.
> You said a typical job is worth about **[their number]**. And you said you could handle about
> **[their capacity]** more a month.
>
> Say we get you **[a deliberately modest number]** qualified leads a month. If you close even one
> in four, that's **[X]** jobs, so about **[X times their job value]** in work.
>
> Here's what that costs you. **[per-lead fee]** a lead, so **[leads × fee]**. That's it from me,
> there's no retainer on top. And your ad budget, which goes straight to Google on your own card,
> call it **[budget]**. So roughly **[total]** out, against **[revenue]** in.
>
> Does that maths look right to you?"

**Four rules here:**
1. **Use a modest lead number.** Undersell it. If you say 40 leads and deliver 15 you have lost the
   client. If you say 10 and deliver 15 you have one for years.
2. **Never say the word "guarantee."** You are showing what the numbers have to look like, not
   promising them.
3. **Ask "does that look right"** and wait. If they argue the close rate down, use their number.
   It is still going to work, and now it is their maths and not yours.
4. **Say "there's no retainer on top" out loud.** It is the single biggest difference between you
   and everyone else they will talk to, and it is the exact thing that lost the Stencil deal when
   it was not true. Do not let them assume the old shape.

**If their lead number lands below the minimum**, say so plainly rather than hiding it:

> "Some months that maths won't clear the minimum, and then you just pay the **[minimum]**. That's
> the floor, it's not on top. In a good month you pay the lead fees instead and I'd rather have the
> good month, because that's the one that means it's working."

### 14 to 19 — What you actually do

Short. They do not want the tour, they want to know it is handled.

> "Here's what happens if we start. I build the campaign, I build a page for the ad to point at
> instead of sending people to your homepage, and I set up tracking so we both know which ads
> actually produced a call. Leads come to you by text and email the second they come in.
>
> Two things I do differently. First, the ad account is **yours**. It's in your name, Google bills
> your card, and if you ever fire me you keep the account and the history. Nothing's held hostage.
> Second, I charge per lead, so if the ads don't produce, that part of my invoice doesn't either."

Those two are true, they are unusual, and they are the whole differentiation. Say them on every
call.

### 19 to 24 — Price, then stop talking

Give one recommendation. Not three options. Three options makes them shop instead of decide.

> "Based on what you told me I'd start you on the **[package]**. It's **$[setup]** one time to
> build everything. Then each month it's **$[fee]** per qualified lead, with a **$[minimum]**
> minimum. You pay whichever of those is higher, never both. Your ad budget is separate and goes
> straight to Google on your own card, and for your market I'd start around **$[budget]** a month.
>
> I've got room to start one new client this week. Want me to get you going?"

**Say the "whichever is higher, never both" line every time.** Prospects have been quoted a
retainer plus a fee by every other agency, so they will hear yours that way unless you stop them.

**Then be quiet.** Whoever speaks first loses. Count to ten in your head if you have to.

### 24 to 30 — Objections and the paperwork

Objection answers are below. When they say yes:

> "Great. I'll send the agreement over in the next few minutes, it's a 3 month term to start so
> there's time for the ads to actually learn. Sign it whenever it lands and I'll get started on the
> build tomorrow. I'll also send a short list of what I need from you: access to your Google
> Business Profile, a few photos of your work, and where you want leads sent."

**Do not end the call without a signed agreement or a booked follow-up with a date and time.**
"I'll get back to you" is a no with extra steps.

## The downsell: Launch & Hand Off

> **⚠️ NOT ADVERTISED.** It is not on the website, not in the recommender, not in any email.
> It exists so you have something honest to offer when a monthly plan is the wrong answer.
> **Full step-by-step for delivering one: `docs/HAND-OFF-RUNBOOK.md`.**

**Use it when the honest answer is "a monthly plan will not work for you."** That is any of:
their ad budget is under $500, they cannot commit to three months, or they have said no to
the monthly twice and you believe them.

**Do NOT lead with it.** It is a smaller sale, and offering it early gives a prospect who
could afford managed an easy way to spend less and get less. Managed makes them more money,
so managed is the recommendation until it plainly is not.

> "Let me offer you something different, because I don't think the monthly plan is right
> for you and I'd rather say that than sell it to you anyway.
>
> At your budget there isn't enough data coming through for me to optimize anything
> meaningfully month to month. You'd be paying me to watch a campaign that doesn't have
> enough to learn from. So instead: I build the whole thing once, properly. The campaign,
> a landing page, call tracking, all of it in your account, in your name.
>
> I run it for the first 30 days and fix what needs fixing. Then I write you a plain
> English playbook, we do a call so you know exactly what to look at each week, and it's
> yours. **$1,500, one time. No monthly, no per-lead fee, nothing after that.**
>
> And if you grow into it and want me running it later, the setup fee comes off, because
> you already paid me to build it."

**Say who hosts the page, on the call, before they ask.**

> "One thing so there are no surprises later. The landing page ends up on your hosting,
> not mine. I hand you the file and a step by step guide, it takes about three minutes and
> it's free. That's deliberate. It means nothing I own can switch off and take your page
> down with it."

That is a genuine advantage over most agencies, where the page dies the day you stop
paying. Do not let it sound like a limitation.

**The three things that make this close.**
1. **You are turning down money.** Saying "the monthly isn't right for you" after they were
   ready to hear a pitch is the most credible thing you will say all call.
2. **The number is smaller than three months of managed.** $1,500 against $1,950. If they
   do the maths out loud, let them.
3. **The waiver removes the risk.** Nothing is wasted if they come back.

### "Why is the one-time build more than your setup fee?"

Fair question, answer it straight.

> "Because the setup fee on a monthly plan is deliberately cheap. I make it back over the
> months you stay. Here there are no months, so the build has to pay for itself. You're
> also getting more than a setup: the first 30 days of me fixing things, and the playbook
> and the call so you can actually run it."

### "Can I just do the one-time and then hire you monthly later?"

> "That's exactly how it's meant to work, and if you do it within six months I'll waive the
> setup fee. You already paid me to build it, so charging you twice for the same campaign
> would be a bit rich."

### Walk away from a hand-off too if

- **They want the price of a hand-off and the service of a plan.** Say no once, plainly.
  Doing free management on a one-time build teaches them the wrong thing and costs you the
  months you should have been paid for.
- **They will not answer the phone.** Same rule as managed. A campaign they never respond
  to fails, and they hand it back to you as your fault.

## Objections

### "How do I know this will work? Who else have you done this for?"

The one that decides whether you are trustworthy. Answer it head on. Never dodge.

> "I'll be straight with you. You'd be one of my first clients. Here's why I think that's actually
> in your favour, and then you can decide.
>
> You're not one of forty accounts to me, you're one of two, so you get my full attention. The ad
> account is in your name so you're not locked in. And I charge per lead, which means if I don't
> produce, I don't get paid for it. I'm not asking you to trust a testimonial. I'm asking you to
> look at the numbers we just ran."

**Never** invent a client, a result, or a "typical client sees" number. One made-up figure and the
whole thing collapses the first time they ask which client.

### "It's too expensive"

Find out which number they mean before answering. Usually it is the setup fee.

> "Which part feels heavy, the monthly or the setup? … Fair enough. Let me put it against the
> maths we just did. You said a job's worth **[their number]**. The setup is less than **[one job /
> half a job]**. So it pays for itself the first time this brings you one customer. After that it's
> **[monthly]** a month against work worth a lot more than that. Is it the number that's the
> problem, or is it that you're not sure it'll work?"

That last question is the important one. **"Too expensive" is almost never about price, it is about
risk.** Once they say "I'm not sure it'll work" you are back to the previous objection, which you
have an honest answer for.

### "I need to think about it"

> "Totally fair. Can I ask what specifically you want to think about? Because if it's the money I
> get it, and if it's whether the thing works I'd rather answer that now than have you sitting on
> it."

Then handle whatever the real objection is. If they still want time, **book the follow-up before
you hang up**, with a date and time.

### "I tried Google ads before and it didn't work"

Good news. This one is easy and it builds trust.

> "What happened? … Yeah, that's the usual story. Two things normally cause it. The ads point at
> the homepage, so someone who searched for one specific thing lands on a page about everything and
> leaves. And nobody's tracking which searches actually produced a call, so you end up paying for
> the wrong clicks for months. Both of those are the first things I fix."

### "Can you guarantee me X leads?"

> "No, and I'd be careful with anyone who does. What I can tell you is what we'll be watching and
> when. If the numbers aren't going the right way by week three, we change the approach, and you'll
> see that in the report rather than hearing it from me after the fact."

### "So am I paying the monthly AND the per-lead?"

**No, and this is the whole point.** Answer it flatly, then stop.

> "No. Whichever is higher, and that's it. If your leads add up to more than the minimum, you pay
> the leads and the minimum doesn't exist. If it's a slow month and they don't, you pay the
> minimum and nothing else. It's never both."

### "Then what's the minimum for?"

> "It's what keeps me able to do the work in a month that doesn't go well. The campaign still gets
> managed, the page still gets updated, the reports still get built. What it isn't is a retainer,
> because the second your leads clear it, it goes away."

If they push further, give them the real reason and let them judge it:

> "Honestly, it's also what stops me taking on a client I can't do a good job for. If your budget
> can't produce enough leads to clear the minimum, that tells us both something before we start
> rather than three months in."

### "Can you just handle the ad spend and bill me one number?"

**Say no.** This is a hard rule, not a preference.

> "I don't do that, on purpose. Your ad account stays in your name with your card on it, so you can
> see every dollar Google charges and there's no markup hiding in the middle. It also means if we
> ever part ways you keep the account and everything it's learned. It's cleaner for both of us."

### "Send me a proposal and I'll look it over"

> "Happy to send everything in writing. Before I do, is there anything in what I've laid out that
> doesn't work for you? I'd rather fix it now than have you read it and think about it for a week."

## Walk away if any of these are true

Saying no to a bad client is worth more than the fee, especially when you only have a handful.

- **They can't handle more work.** More leads make an already-drowning business worse, they will
  blame you, and they will churn.
- **They won't spend at least $500 a month on ads.** That is the hard floor. Below it there is not
  enough data for the campaign to learn, so it fails and it is your name on it. Say it kindly and
  tell them to come back when they can.
- **They want Google and Meta on less than $5,000 a month.** Not a no to the deal, a no to that
  shape of it. Put the whole budget behind one channel and add the second when the numbers earn
  it. Split small and neither side learns.
- **They want you to front or absorb the ad spend.** Never. This is a hard business rule.
- **They want a guarantee** after you have explained why you do not give one.
- **They don't answer their phone.** Ads cannot fix a business that misses calls. Tell them that
  plainly, and offer to talk again once they have someone answering.

## Same day, after the call

1. Send the agreement (from the OS).
2. Send a short recap email: what you agreed, what it costs, what you need from them.
3. Add them to the OS with their package and their real numbers.
4. If they did not sign: log the actual objection in their notes. Not "wants to think", the real
   one.

---

## The three habits that decide the call

1. **Talk less than half the time.** If you are talking more than they are, you are pitching, not
   selling.
2. **Use their number, not yours.** Every time you can say "you told me…" you get stronger.
3. **Ask for the business out loud.** Most calls are lost because nobody actually asked.
